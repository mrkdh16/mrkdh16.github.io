Course Syllabus — Fall 2026

Learning Mechanics

Towards a Scientific Theory of Deep Learning

Mark Rhee UC Berkeley [Room / Time TBD]

Course Description

This seminar-style course explores the emerging science of “learning mechanics”—the first-principles, mathematical study of how neural networks learn. Drawing heavily from the whitepaper “There Will Be a Scientific Theory of Deep Learning” (Simon et al., 2026) and the primary literature it synthesizes, we will examine the theoretical tools, empirical regularities, and open questions that are laying the groundwork for a physics-like understanding of deep learning.

The course is organized around five lines of evidence that a scientific theory is possible: (1) analytically solvable settings, (2) insightful limits, (3) macroscopic laws, (4) hyperparameter disentanglement, and (5) universality across systems. We will also explore the relationship between learning mechanics and mechanistic interpretability.

Prerequisites

  • Linear algebra and multivariate calculus at the level of Math 53, 54/110
  • Probability and statistics at the level of EECS 126 / Data 140 / Stat 134
  • Working knowledge of neural networks and deep learning at the level of CS 189
  • Comfort with Python and PyTorch (for homework and the final project)

It is highly recommended that you bring a “physicist’s mindset” to the table. This means you should be comfortable with the scientific method as it applies to complex systems: formulating testable hypotheses, checking theoretical predictions against empirical measurements, solving simplified cases first, and using educated guesses (ansätze) to solve equations. We care more about simple, intuitive insights and tight connections between theory and experiment than we do about technically true mathematics.

Learning Objectives

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Articulate what a scientific theory of deep learning would look like and why it matters.
  2. Derive and analyze learning dynamics in solvable settings (deep linear networks, kernel regression).
  3. Explain the lazy/rich dichotomy and its implications for feature learning.
  4. Critically evaluate empirical scaling laws and proposed explanations.
  5. Design and execute small-scale experiments that test theoretical predictions about neural network training.
  6. Identify and discuss open problems at the frontier of learning mechanics.

Attendance & Participation (10%)

The liveliness of this course heavily depends on in-class participation. Please show up to class and ask questions! Two unexcused absences are allowed without penalty.

Reading Questions (20%)

For every paper we read, please generate 2–3 questions and submit them before class.

Homework Assignments (40%)

Three to four problem sets will be distributed over the semester. These will mix analytical derivations with computational exercises. Collaboration is encouraged but each student must write up their own solutions.

Final Project (30%)

The final project is the capstone of the course. Working individually or in pairs, you will:

  • Identify a phenomenon related to topics covered in class (or for the especially ambitious, a related open problem from the whitepaper).
  • Formulate a testable hypothesis about that phenomenon.
  • Design and run an experiment to test the hypothesis (computational experiments on small models are perfectly appropriate).
  • Present and write up your findings, including whether the hypothesis was supported, what you learned, and what questions remain.

Weekly Schedule

Each session is 2 hours long. A typical class includes a 50–60 minute lecture/discussion of the reading, a 10-minute break, and a 40–50 minute deep-dive on derivations, experiments, or open questions.


1
First Week: No Class
2
Introduction to Learning Mechanics
Simon et al. (2026), “There Will Be a Scientific Theory of Deep Learning”
Big idea: The physicist’s approach to studying deep learning. What do we mean by a scientific theory of deep learning? The seven desiderata. Overview of the five lines of evidence. Why theory matters for engineering and safety.
3
Analytically Solvable Settings I: Deep Linear Networks
Saxe, McClelland & Ganguli (2014), “Exact Solutions to the Nonlinear Dynamics of Learning in Deep Linear Neural Networks” + Mark’s blog post
Big idea: Analyzing models that are linear in the data—f is a linear function of x. Gradient flow decouples into solvable Bernoulli ODEs. Sequential learning of singular modes and greedy low-rank bias. Gain insight into the role of depth.
4
Analytically Solvable Settings II: The NTK and Kernel Regression
Lee et al. (2019) or Jacot, Gabriel & Hongler (2018), “Neural Tangent Kernel” + Lil’Log blog post
Big idea: Analyzing models that are linear in the parameters—flin is a linear function of the parameters of f, and flin ≈ f for large width. Kernel regression as a tractable model for wide neural nets.
5
Analytically Solvable Settings III: Eigenlearning and the HEA Jamie / Joey guest lecture (?)
Simon et al. (2023), “The Eigenlearning Framework” + Karkada et al. (2025), “Predicting Kernel Regression Learning Curves from Only Raw Data Statistics”
Big idea: Predict expected generalization error of KRR by accounting for input data structure. Apply the eigenframework to realistic data distributions with the Heuristic Eigenlearning Ansatz. Real data distribution → eigenframework → predict generalization error.
6
Insightful Limits: Lazy vs. Rich Dichotomy and μP
Chizat, Oyallon & Bach (2019), “On Lazy Training in Differentiable Programming” + Yang et al. (2022), “Tensor Programs V”
Big idea: When and why does feature learning happen? Infinite-width limits and the lazy/rich dichotomy. μP as the Maximal Update Parameterization that enables hyperparameter transfer. A triumph of theory impacting practice.
7
Macroscopic Regularities: Neural Collapse & the Neural Feature Ansatz
Papyan, Han & Donoho (2020), “Prevalence of Neural Collapse” + Radhakrishnan et al. (2024), “Mechanism for Feature Learning in Neural Networks”
Big idea: Coarse-grained picture of feature learning. Neural collapse: simplex geometry of class means. The neural feature ansatz: first-layer weight alignment with average gradient outer products. Building theory top-down from empirical regularities.
8
Universality: The Platonic Representation Hypothesis & Fourier Features Dhruva guest lecture (?)
Huh et al. (2024), “The Platonic Representation Hypothesis” + Karkada et al. (2026), “Symmetry in Language Statistics Shapes the Geometry of Model Representations”
Big idea: Universal convergence of representations across architectures and modalities. How translational symmetry in language statistics provably induces Fourier features in word embeddings—and we see exactly this in LLMs. A tight theory-empirics connection exemplifying learning mechanics.
9
Learning Mechanics ↔ Mechanistic Interpretability
Simon et al. (2026), Section 4 + Welch Labs video on grokking
Big idea: The physics–biology analogy. How learning mechanics formalizes mech interp assumptions (linearity, sparsity, compositionality). How mech interp discoveries drive theory. Connection between grokking and Dhruva’s translational symmetry → Fourier features idea.
10
Edge of Stability and Optimization Phenomena
Cohen et al. (2021), “Gradient Descent on Neural Networks Typically Occurs at the Edge of Stability”
Big idea: Progressive sharpening and the 2/η threshold. Implicit curvature regularization by optimizers. Central flows and curvature-penalized gradient flow. How hyperparameters modulate curvature penalties.
11
Beyond Linearization: Nonlinear Toy Models
Bordelon, Atanasov & Pehlevan (2025), “How Feature Learning Can Improve Neural Scaling Laws” or Morwani et al. (2023), “Feature Emergence via Margin Maximization”
Big idea: Moving beyond linearized/kernel models. Teacher–student models and multi-index models. Feature learning improving sample complexity. Modular arithmetic and grokking as test beds. The frontier of genuinely nonlinear solvable models.
12
Neural Scaling Laws
Kaplan et al. (2020), “Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models”
Big idea: Power-law scaling of loss with compute, data, and parameters. Candidate explanations: data manifold dimension, feature superposition, task structure. Why can’t we predict exponents a priori? Compute-optimal training.
13
Implicit Regularization and Simplicity Bias
TBD—candidates include: Blanc et al. (2020), “Implicit Regularization for Deep Neural Networks”; Kalimeris et al. (2019), “SGD on Neural Networks Learns Functions of Increasing Complexity”; or Cohen et al. (2025), “Understanding Optimization in Deep Learning with Central Flows”
Big idea: Do neural networks implicitly minimize some notion of functional complexity? Simplicity bias, spectral bias, and maximum margin. How optimizers steer toward simpler solutions. Connections to the sparse features and circuits studied by mech interp.
14
Final Project: Hypothesis Presentations
No new reading
Students present their hypotheses, planned experimental setups, and preliminary thinking. Short talks (~5 min each) followed by class feedback and Q&A. ↓ Final report due end of semester.
15
Final Project: Office Hours & Lab Support FLAB members (?)
No new reading
Open office hours for final project work. Get hands-on help with experimental design, debugging, analysis, and interpretation. Lab members available for one-on-one and small-group guidance.

RRR Week: No Class
